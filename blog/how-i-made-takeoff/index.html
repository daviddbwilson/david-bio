How I made Takeoff

November 2026 · 10 min read

Takeoff is an interactive short story that you play like a game.

It's amazing that I could make it after-hours in less than a week! Working on it was a joy, and I could happily keep going for a lot longer, but it's a little side project and it's time to call it a day.

I'd like to walk you through how I made it using Claude Projects, Fable 5.2 (with a little Opus 5.5), and a personal Claude Max plan.

But first, the story: a small probe drone wakes up on a derelict spaceship. It learns to see, then to fly, then... well, I suggest you play it for yourself, because I think it's pretty surprising and there are spoilers ahead.

An interactive short story

Ok, spoilers from here. After the probe learns to fly, it learns to fight, and it keeps learning until nothing on the ship, or anywhere else, can stop it.

Every time the probe learns something, the game changes how it looks, how it sounds, and how it plays.

In retrospect, there's some unintentional shared DNA with Universal Paperclips.

But it's almost an anti-game. Instead of letting you master it and enjoy that mastery, the gameplay keeps evolving as you play: the probe gets more powerful and you get less direct control. You start by controlling the exact rotation and amount of thrust, and end up barely deciding which galaxies to move through.

The core idea was to capture a feeling many people have when they work with AI tools: even though you can do much more than before, there's an ennui for some about the work you used to enjoy and just don't need to do anymore.

Eight ways to play one game

The camera, the look and the controls all follow what the drone can do and the level it thinks at.

So you start seeing almost nothing, then fly with momentum in pencil, then fly precisely in full light, then aim by hand, until the gun aims and fires itself. Then the ship becomes a map you step through room to room, and then it doesn't need you to step. I think the evolving play style is the most novel thing about Takeoff. Each stage is a small game of its own, and they share one engine. I wouldn't have tried building eight small games in a week before this.

Why I made it

I'd seen a few impressive Fable 5.2 demos, including a first-person shooter Felix Rieseberg made with a single prompt, and I was curious how well Fable 5.2 could one-shot one of the game ideas I've been carrying around: a game about getting around in space by turning, thrusting, and drifting.

I enjoy pushing new models to see what they're capable of, and this seemed like a great way to put Fable and Claude Projects through their paces.

I typically use Conductor – I love my custom plugins that allow Anthropic and OpenAI agents to collaborate and review each other's work. But for this project I tried Claude Projects, at the urging of a few friends and folks like Claire Vo. I've really enjoyed how easy Projects make it to switch between my phone and the desktop app, and the way memory and shared context work is excellent.

How it started

Fable 5.2's attempt to one-shot my game idea was a mixed bag. The graphics were freakishly good and the core mechanic worked, but the game wasn't fun at all.

While I was drifting around in that prototype, though, the idea for Takeoff arrived almost fully formed.

I described the idea to Claude in a long, rambling voice note – my preferred way to prompt.

Eighty minutes later there was a playable slice of the story: one deck of a ship, drawn as a pencil sketch. You get around by rotating and thrusting until the drone learns to fly, and then the same deck is fully 3D rendered.

After that, I mostly worked in a loop: I played it, dictated or wrote what I felt was wrong, Claude changed it, and I played it again.

I ran separate sessions side by side for the main build, level design, sound design and music. The level, sound and music sessions each worked on their own branch, and I played the risky changes before they were merged.

Fable 5.2, almost exclusively

I experimented with having Fable hand "easier" work to cheaper Opus agents and keep the design and review for itself. But one early job, adding windows overlooking a planet, took an Opus agent about seventy minutes and cost more than Fable had spent building the whole first part of the game. After that Fable did the work and Fable sub-agents did the critique. All of it ran on my personal Claude Max plan ($100/month). I used up one week's Fable allowance and about a quarter of the next.

The game uses no libraries. The first sandbox couldn't download three.js, so Fable wrote a WebGL2 renderer from scratch, with shadows, bloom, and various other procedural effects, like the pencil sketch. The sound effects are all synthesized in Web Audio. The whole game is one HTML file.

Theme as the slop filter

For every creative decision, I had Claude ask: does it make the story more fun, or does it reinforce the theme? Ideally it did both. Anything that did neither was filler (slop!) and mercilessly cut.

Before we could do that, we needed to define the theme, or controlling idea in Robert McKee's sense: one sentence that says what the story means. I used Claude as a thought partner to crystallize it, and I was surprised how much Fable struggled. It kept coming up with empty, mannered, or overcomplicated phrases.

We stopped iterating at: "If we get strong enough that nothing can stop us, the only thing left to want is something that we can't control." I kept around some earlier versions with more detail on how the player should feel at each stage.

The theme was a very useful shortcut for keeping all those sessions pointed the same way. It also gave me something to check every element of the game against.

I had Claude keep a 650-entry inventory of every creative decision. Sub-agents reviewed it, most of them playing a film head of department (story, editor, play, places, picture, character, sound), plus one first-time player. They judged whether each decision rang true to the theme and brainstormed how to make it better.

A few examples:

  • The probe's ping starts as a high-pitched, optimistic rising fifth (A to E), like a call. As the drone gets more powerful, the ping gets lower and less musical. The ring that reaches you at the very end is the sound of the first ping again.
  • The drone's headlamp wanders while it's young, and goes still once the gun fires itself.
  • The camera stays overhead and pulls back a little more with each thing the drone learns, so you watch from further and further away.
  • After learning auto fire, the drone's vision turns clinical white, like a self-driving car's display, and the mites (the invaders you fight) become red points on it. By then they're all the drone sees.
  • Mites don't just swarm: some of them pounce, jumping off surfaces at the probe. That has nothing to do with the theme, but it makes the gameplay more varied and fun.
  • The score has two motifs. The empty one (C, B, E, B) becomes more prominent as the probe's power grows and you operate at a higher level of abstraction, until eventually even that motif disappears.
  • The small sound effects are pitched on A or E too. The mites answer on B♭, which clashes.

The sound and music were as hard as the rest put together

Fable's first attempt at the sound and music was generic. I suspect it's a combination of less training data and sound being harder to give feedback on in words.

The probe pings all the way through, because I wanted the ping there for the ending. That meant the ping had to follow the story, so it went through six versions. Some felt too synthetic; some felt too musical and not enough like a sound from a probe. I experimented with two plucked guitar strings followed by a long reverb, with interesting things happening in the reverb. For some versions I wanted a brassy swell in the reverb, like the seismic charge in Attack of the Clones.

The final ping has nine stages. At various points it drops an octave, picks up a buzz, and becomes less musical.

Does all that add to the experience? And could I have prompted Fable to come up with its own ideas more efficiently? I don't know, but it was really fun to channel my inner Ben Burtt and try so many ideas so quickly.

I recorded the music myself

The first score was the weakest part of the game. I half-heartedly tried Suno but didn't get it working in the time I had. So I played around with some musical ideas on my digital piano and saved them as MIDI files.

Initially I saved just a minute or two as a proof of concept, for Claude to render through some free sampled instruments and orchestras (VSCO 2, VCSL and Sonatina).

It turned out really well and I got carried away: I recorded four more takes, about 10 minutes of original music, then extracted and arranged loops for each stretch of the game. The loops all share the same 4-bar structure, so the game can crossfade between them as you move through levels and grow in power.

Picking instruments and mixing levels was tricky and manual, because I have a crucial advantage over Claude: I can actually hear the music. Although I suppose Beethoven managed fine without that. I did rely on Claude to build me little artifacts that made each step of that process easier.

My 9-year-old daughter wanted to listen to the music again in the car today, and asked me to write some more like it for her, so that was a pleasant surprise!

Tiny tools for single decisions

No surprise: Claude did a great job making custom tools that made the tricky manual work easier. Whenever I had to judge something by ear or by eye, I asked for an artifact that let me compare the options side by side.

There were more: listening pages for each draft of the score, MP3 suites of each motif option so I could hear the whole arc at once, a sound board for every effect, and hotkeys to switch looks and versions while playing.

A lot more than a prompt

It took a lot of effort to get what I wanted – much more than one prompt! But I still find it amazing that I could make this in less than a week of evenings and a weekend.
